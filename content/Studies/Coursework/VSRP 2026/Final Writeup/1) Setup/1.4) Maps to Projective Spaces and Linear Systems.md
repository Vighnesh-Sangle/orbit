### 1.4) Maps to Projective Spaces and Linear Systems

#### Maps to Projective spaces coming from line bundles

> [!orangebox]+ **Theorem 1.4.1**: (Hartshorne II 7.1)
> Let $A$ be a ring and let $X$ be a scheme over $A$.
>
> 1. If $\varphi: X\to \PP^n_A$ is an $A$-morphism, then $\varphi^*(\SO(1))$ is an invertible sheaf on $X$ generated by global sections $s_i = \varphi^*(x_i)$
> 2. Conversely, if $\SL$ is an invertible sheaf with on $X$, and if $s_0, s_1,\dots,s_n\in \Gamma(X,\SL)$ are global sections which generate $X$, then there exists a unique $A$ morphism $\varphi:X \to \PP^n_A$ such that $\SL \simeq \varphi^*(\SO(1))$ under this isomorphism

^42663f

Consider what happens in the case of varieties.
Let $X$ be a variety over $k$.
Given a global section of $s$ of a line-bundle $\SL$, there is a notion of evaluating it at a closed point. Fix an isomorphism $\SL_x \simeq \OXx$ and under this isomorphism, $s \mapsto f$. Set $s(x) = f(x)$

This is of course not a well-defined notion, since the isomorphism $\SL_x\to \OXx$ is not a unique one. But it makes sense to ask when $s(x) = 0$. This will come up in the definition of [[#^fcda89|divisor of zeros]].

Even though the function itself is not well-defined, we may still talk about maps to $\PP^n$ using this. Given a set of generating global sections $s_0, \dots, s_n$, on the closed points, the map of [[#^42663f|Theorem]] 1.4.1 can be described as $x \mapsto (s_0(x):s_1(x):\dots:s_n(x))$. Here we fix an isomorphism $\SL_x\to \OXx$ and evaluate all $s_i(x)$ via this isomorphism.

This is a well defined notion. Since if we choose a different isomorphism, it will induce an isomorphism $\OXx \to \OXx$ and this will be multiplication by a unit, say $g$. Then under this map, $x \mapsto (g(x)s_0(x):g(x)s_1(x):\dots:g(x)s_n(x))$ and since $g$ is a unit in $\OXx$, $g(x) \neq 0$, thus this is same as $(s_0(x):s_1(x):\dots:s_n(x))$

> [!orangebox]+ **Theorem 1.4.2**: (Hartshorne II 7.3)
> Let $k$ be an algebraically closed field, let $X$ be a projective scheme over $k$, let $\varphi: X\to \PP^n_k$ be a morphism over $k$, corresponding to $\SL$ and $s_0,\dots, s_n\in  \Gamma(X,\SL)$ as above. Let $V \subseteq \Gamma(X,\SL)$ be the subspace spanned by the $s_i$. Then $\varphi$ is a closed immersion if and only if
>
> 1. elements of $V$ separate points, i.e., for any two distinct closed points $P,Q\in X$, there is an $s \in V$ such that $s \in \mathfrak m_P\SL_P$ but $s \notin \mathfrak m_Q\SL_Q$ or vice versa.
> 2. elements of $V$ separate tangent vectors, i.e. for each closed points $P\in X$, the set $\set{s\in V| s_P\in\mathfrak m_P\SL_P}$ spans the $k$-vector space $\mathfrak m_P\SL_P/\mathfrak m_P^2\SL_P$

#### Linear Systems

For this section, we only focus on non-singular projective varieties over an algebraically closed field.

For such varieties, [[Studies/Coursework/VSRP 2026/Final Writeup/1) Setup/1.2) Cartier Divisors#^a2f5ac|Proposition]] 1.2.10 applies and we talk about Weil and Cartier Divisors interchangeably. Further, since varieties are integral schemes, [[Studies/Coursework/VSRP 2026/Final Writeup/1) Setup/1.3) Invertible Sheaves#^6085e5|Proposition]] 1.3.9 also applies.

> [!yellowbox]+ **Definition 1.4.3:** (Complete Linear System $|D|$)
> For a divisor $D$ on a non-singular projective variety, $|D|$ is the set of all effective divisors linearly equivalent to $D$

> [!yellowbox]+ **Definition/Proposition 1.4.4:** (Divisor of zeroes $(s)_0$)
> Let $\SL$ be a line bundle and $s \in \Gamma(X,\SL)$ a global section.
> $\SL$ can be covered by $\set {U_i}$ where $\SL|_{U_i} \simeq \SO_{U_i}$ and under the isomorphism $s|_{U_i} \mapsto s_i \in \SO_{U_i}$
> $(s)_0$ is the divisor that corresponds to the Cartier divisor given by $\set{(s_i,U_i)}$, equivalently the Weil divisor given by coefficient of a prime divisor $Z$ being $\nu_Z(s_i)$ if $Z$ intersects $U_i$.
> \
> This is a well-defined notion.

^fcda89

> [!proof]- _Proof:_
> Suppose that $\SL$ can also be covered by $V_j$ such that $\SL|_{V_J}\simeq \SO_{V_J}$ and under this isomorphism $s|_{V_j} \mapsto t_j$. So, the Cartier divisor would be given by $\set{(t_j,V_j)}$. It is enough to show that the two Cartier divisors are the same.
> \
> The two Cartier divisors would be equal $\iff$ $s_i/t_j \in \OX^\times(U_i\cap V_j)$ for each $i,j$
> \
> The first isomorphism $\SL|_{U_i} \simeq \SO_{U_i}$ gives us an isomorphism $\SL(U_i\cap V_j) \simeq \OX(U_i\cap V_j)$ where $s|_{U_i\cap V_j} \mapsto s_i$
> Similarly, the second isomorphism $\SL|_{V_j}\simeq \SO_{V_j}$ yields another isomorphism $\SL(U_i\cap V_j) \simeq \OX(U_i\cap V_j)$ where $s|_{U_i\cap V_j} \mapsto t_j$
> \
> Chaining these, we get an automorphism of $\OX(U_i\cap V_j)$ which sends $s_i$ to $t_j$. But since it is an automorphism as an $\OX(U_i\cap V_j)$ module, it is obtained by multiplication by a unit. Thus $t_j = as_i$ where $a\in \OX^\times(U_i\cap V_j)$. Thus $s_i/t_j\in \OX^\times(U_i\cap V_j)$ as required.

> [!bluebox] **Proposition 1.4.5:** (Hartshorne II 7.7)
> Let $X$ be a nonsingular projective variety over an algebraically closed field $k$. Let $D_0$ be a divisor on $X$ and let $\SL\simeq \SL(D_0)$ be the corresponding invertible sheaf. Then:
>
> 1. For each non-zero $s\in \Gamma(X, \SL)$ the divisor $(s)_0$ is an effective divisor linearly equivalent to $D_0$
> 2. Every effective divisor linearly equivalent to $D_0$ is $(s)_0$ for some $s\in \Gamma(X, \SL$
> 3. any two section $s,s' \in \Gamma(X, \SL)$ have the same divisor of zeros if an only if $s = \lambda s'$ for some $\lambda \in k^\times$

^d89021

In the proof of Proposition 1.4.5, we show that if $s \mapsto f \in K(X$) under the isomorphism to $\SL(D_0)$ then $(s)_0 = D_0 + (f)$.

A sort of converse of the above statement also holds

> [!greenbox]+ **Lemma 1.4.6**:
> Let $X$ be a nonsingular projective variety over an algebraically closed field $k$. Let $D_0$ be a divisor on $X$.
> Then $D_0 + (f)$ is an effective divisor $\iff$ $f\in \Gamma(X,\SL(D_0))$

> [!proof]- _Proof_:
> The backward implication is shown in the proof of [[#^d89021|Proposition]] 1.4.5.
> For the forward, note that if $D_0$ is given as $\set{(f_i, U_i)}$ then $D + (f)$ will be given as $\set{(ff_i, U_i)}$ and by hypothesis this is effective. Thus $ff_i \in \OX(U_i) \implies f \in \frac 1 {f_i} \OX(U_i) = \SL(D_0)(U_i)$
> \
> Since this holds for all $U_i$, we get that $f \in \Gamma(X, \SL(D))$>

From [[#^d89021|Proposition]] 1.4.5, we see that the complete linear system $|D_0|$ is in bijection with the  set $\set{\Gamma(X,\SL) - 0}/k^\times$. And by [[Studies/Coursework/VSRP 2026/Final Writeup/2) Sheaf Cohomological Results/2) Sheaf Cohomological Results#^8f9d0b|Theorem]] 2.3, $\Gamma(X,\SL)$ is a finite dimensional $k$-vector s pace. Thus $|D_0|$ has the structure of the closed points of a projective-n-space over $k$.

From this we get the definition of a linear system.

> [!yellowbox]+ **Definition 1.4.7**: (Linear system)
> Let $X$ be a nonsingular projective variety over an algebraically closed field $k$ and $D$ a divisor on $X$.
> A linear system $\mathfrak d$ is linear subspace of $|D|$ (where $|D|$ is given the structure of closed points of $\PP^n$ via [[#^d89021|Proposition]] 1.4.5)
> \
> Equivalently, it corresponds to a $k$-subspace of $V\subseteq \Gamma(X, \SL(D))$

> [!yellowbox]+ **Definition 1.4.8**: (dimension of a linear system)
> Dimension of a linear system $\mathfrak d$ is its dimension an projective linear subspace. Thus $\Dim \mathfrak d = \Dims k V -1$

#### Maps to Projective Spaces from Linear Systems

> [!yellowbox]+ **Definition 1.4.9**: (Base point of a linear system)
> A point $p\in X$ is said to be a base point of a linear system $\mathfrak d$ if for all divisors $D \in \mathfrak d$, we have $p \in \Supp D$

> [!bluebox]+ **Lemma 1.4.10**: (Hartshorne II 7.8)
> Let $X$ be a nonsingular projective variety and $\mathfrak d$ be a linear system corresponding to $V \subseteq \Gamma(X, \SL)$.
> The $p\in X$ is  a base point of $\mathfrak d \iff s_p \in \mathfrak m_p\SL_p \fall s \in V$

^1e5dd5

A consequence of [[#^1e5dd5|lemma]] 1.4.10 is that if a linear system $\mathfrak d$ is base point free, then the corresponding $\SL$ is generated by global section in $V$.

The following reframes [[#^42663f|Theorem]] 1.4.1 in terms of linear systems

> [!bluebox]+ **Proposition 1.4.11**: (Hartshorne II Remark 7.8.1)
> Let $X$ be a nonsingular projective variety. Then giving a map to $\PP^n_k$ is equivalent to giving a base point free linear system $\mathfrak d$ of dimenion $n$
> \
> More precisely, giving a map $\varphi: X \to \PP^n_k$ is $k$ morphism, then $\varphi^*(\SO(1)) = \SL$ is a line bundle on $X$ and $\varphi^*(\Gamma(\PP^n_k,\SO(1))) \subseteq \Gamma(X,\SL)$ corresponds to a base point free linear system
> \
> And given a line bundle $\SL$ and a base point free linear system $\mathfrak d$ corresponding to $V\subseteq \Gamma(X,\SL)$, there exists a $k$-morphism $\varphi: X \to \PP^n_k$ such that $\varphi^*(\SO(1)) \simeq \SL$ and $\varphi^*(\Gamma(\PP^n_k,\SO(1))) = V$

^5c87fa

Fixing a basis $s_0,\dots, s_n$ for $V$, from [[#^1e5dd5|lemma]] 1.4.10 we get that these global sections generate $\SL$ and then by [[#^42663f|Theorem]] 1.4.1 we get the maps.

The  following reframes [[#^00e143|Theorem]] 1.4.2 in terms of linear systems

> [!bluebox] **Proposition 1.4.12:** (Hartshorne II Remark 7.8.2)
> Let $X$ be a nonsingular projective variety, $\mathfrak d$ a linear system corresponding to $V\subseteq \Gamma(X,\SL)$. Then the map from coming from this linear system via [[#^5c87fa|Proposition]] 1.4.11 is a closed embedding if and only if
>
> 1. $\mathfrak d$ separated points, i.e., for any two distinct closed points $P,Q \in X$, there is a $D \in \mathfrak d$ such that $P \in \Supp D$ and $Q \notin \Supp D$ or vice versa.
> 2. $\mathfrak d$ separates tangent vectors. Given any closed point $P$ and a tangent vector $t \in T_p(X)$ there is a $D \in \mathfrak d$ such that $P \in \Supp D$ but $t \notin T_P(Y_D) \subseteq T_p(X)$ where $Y_D$ is the closed subscheme corresponding to the effective divisor $D$, coming from [[Studies/Coursework/VSRP 2026/Final Writeup/1) Setup/1.3) Invertible Sheaves#Ideal sheaves corresponding to effective divisors|here]]

#### Explicitly describing the pulled-back linear system

We have morphism of non-singular varieties, $f: X\to Y$ and $\phi: \SO_Y \to f_*\OX$.
$\SL$ a line bundle on $Y$ and $\mathfrak d$ a linear system corresponding to $V\subseteq \Gamma(Y,\SL)$

$f^*\SL$ is a line bundle on $X$. If $V$ is open subset of $Y$ such that $\SL|_{V} \simeq \SO_V$,
Then $f^*\SL|_{f\inv(V)} \simeq \SO_X|_{f\inv (V)}$.
We also have a canonical map $f^*: \Gamma(V,\SL) \to \Gamma(f\inv(V), f^*\SL)$, i.e. a map $\SO_Y(V) \to \OX(f\inv (V))$
This map is same as $\phi$.

This is because the map is $s \mapsto 1\otimes s \subseteq \OX \otimes_{f\inv \SO_Y} f\inv \SO_Y$. And the algebra structure on $\OX$ comes from $f\inv\SO_Y \to \OX$ corresponding to $\phi$.
So $\SO_Y(V) \to f\inv\SO_Y(f\inv (V)) \to \OX(f\inv (V))$ is same as $\phi(V)$ and thus $1\otimes s \leftrightarrow \phi(s)\in \OX(f\inv (V))$

Now suppose $s\in V$ and $(s)_0$ the divisor of zeros of $s$.
Let $U_i$ be a cover on which $\SL$ is trivial and get isomorphisms $\SL|_{U_i} \simeq \SO_{U_i}$ and $f^*\SL|_{f\inv(U_i)} \simeq \SO_{f\inv(U_i)}$.
If $s|_{U_i} \leftrightarrow f_i$ under the isomorphisms, then $(s)_0$ corresponds to the Cartier divisor $(f_i,U_i)$
We want to see what the divisor of zeros of $f^*(s)$ is.

From the discussion above, it is clear that for the chosen trivialization of $f^*\SL$, we get that $f^*(s)|_{f\inv U_i} \leftrightarrow \phi(f_i)$. If any of the $\varphi(f_i)$ is zero, then $f^*(s)$ is zero on an open set and we must have that $f^*(s) = 0$. So talking about the divisor of zeros doesn't make sense.

If $f^*(s) \neq 0$, then we must have $\phi(f_i)$ non-zero for each $i$, and so $(\phi(f_i), f\inv(U_i))$ is a valid Cartier divisor on $X$.

So we get our final result:

> [!greenbox] Divisor of zero of pullback
> Let $(f, \phi): X\to Y$ be a morphism of non-singular varieties. $\SL$ a line bundle on $Y$, $f^*: \Gamma(Y, \SL) \to \Gamma(X, f^*\SL)$. Then if for $s\in \Gamma(Y,\SL)$, $f^*(s) \neq 0$ and the divisor of zeros of $s$ as a Cartier divisor can be described as $(f_i, U_i)$, then the divisor of zeros of $f^*(s)$ corresponds to the Cartier divisor $(\phi(f_i), f\inv (U_i))$.

**Remark:** If $f$ was a dominant map of integral schemes, then the above tells us $(f^*(s))_0 = f^*((s)_0)$.

**Remark:** We worked in non-singular varieties because we will mainly use this result in the context of linear systems and we want to think of the divisor of zeros as a Weil divisor. If we let $X,Y$ be arbitrary integral schemes, and the divisor of zeros as simply a Cartier divisor, then the above would still hold. (being integral is needed to determine when $f^*(s)$ is zero easily, but otherwise can be dropped as well).

#### Rational Functions as Maps to $\PP^1$

> [!greenbox] **Lemma 1.4.13:**
> Let $X$ be variety. $f \in K(X)$ such that for all closed points $x \in X$, either $f \in \OXx$ or $\frac 1 f \in \OXx$. Then there exists a morphism $\tilde f: X\to \PP^1$ such that:
> On closed points $x$ where $f$ is defined, $\tilde f (x) = (f(x):1)$ and
> On closed points $x$ where $\frac 1 f$ is defined, $\tilde f(x) = (1: \frac 1 f(x))$
> \
> Conversely, all maps $\varphi:X\to \PP^1$ are of this form, i.e., $\exists \ f\in K(X)$ such that $\varphi = \tilde f$
> \
> Further, if $f$ is a non-constant rational function, then $\tilde f$ is dominant and the induced map $k(\frac {x_0} {x_1}) \to K(X)$ is $\frac {x_0} {x_1} \mapsto f$

^dacdae

> [!proof]- _Proof:_
> That this map is regular can be checked directly, but I will use [[#^42663f|Theorem]] 1.4.1
> \
> Let $U$ be the open of points such that $f \in \OXx$ and $V$ be the open set of points such that $\frac 1 f \in \OXx$. By hypothesis, $X = U \cup V$
> \
> Consider the Cartier divisor given by $D =\set {(f,U), (1,V)}$
> $\SL(D)$ is locally described as $\set {(\frac 1 f \SO_U, U), (\SO_V, V)}$
> $\SL(D)$ has global sections $1, \frac 1 f$. $1$ generates $\SL(D)_x$ for all $x \in V$ and $\frac 1 f$ generates $\SL(D)_x$ for all $x \in U$. So we have a map to $\PP^1$ coming from these sections.
> \
> On closed points, for $x \in U$, we can fix the isomorphism $\frac 1 f \SO_U \to \SO_U$ to be multiplication by $f$. So $1\mapsto f$ and $\frac 1 f \mapsto 1$ and the map is $x \mapsto (f(x): 1)$
> Similarly, it can be seen that taking the identity as the isomorphism for $V$, we get the required map on $V$
> \
> Now, to show that all maps are of this form, let $\varphi: X\to \PP^1$ be such a map.
> Not that $U' = \PP^1 - \set {(1:0)} \simeq \AA^1$ via $(a:b) \mapsto \frac a b$ and $V' = \PP^1- \set{(0:1)} \simeq \AA^1$ via $(a:b) \mapsto\frac b a$
> Let $U = \varphi\inv(U')$ and $V = \varphi\inv(V')$. $X = U\cup V$
> $\varphi|_U$ is a map from $U$ to $\AA^1$, thus corresponds to $f \in \OX(U) \subseteq K(X)$
> Similarly, $\varphi|_V$ corresponds to $g \in \OX(V) \subseteq K(X)$
> \
> On $U\cap V$, if $x$ is a closed point, $(f(x):1) = \varphi(x) = (1: g(x))$. And here both $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ are non-zero since $\varphi(x) \in U'\cap V'$. So we get $f(x) = \frac 1 {g(x)}$. So $fg = 1$ on $U\cap V$and hence $fg = 1$ in $K(X)$. Thus we have $\varphi = \tilde f$
