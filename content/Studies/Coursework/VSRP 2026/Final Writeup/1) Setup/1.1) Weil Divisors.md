### 1.2) Weil Divisors

#### Definitions and First Properties

> [!yellowbox]+ **Definition 1.2.1**: (Regular in Codimension 1)
> A scheme $X$ is said to be regular in codimension 1 if all it's dimension 1 local rings are regulars.

> [!yellowbox]+ Notation:
> We will a scheme a $(*)$  scheme if it is Noetherian, Integral, separated and regular in codimension one.

Note that nonsingular varieties satisfy this hypothesis. Noetherian, integral and separated are clear. Regularity follows since the local rings at maximal ideals are regular, all local rings are regular, in particular the dimension 1 local rings.

Observe that for a $(*)$ scheme $X$ and codimension integral subscheme $Z$, the local for this subscheme, that local ring at its generic point $\eta_Z$ is a noetherian, dimension 1, regular local integral domain. Hence $\SO_{X,\eta_X}$ is a DVR.

Let the corresponding valuation on the field of rational functions $K(X)$ be denoted by $\nu_Z$.

> [!yellowbox]+ **Definition 1.2.2**: (Prime Divisor)
> For a $(*)$ scheme $X$, a prime divisor is a codimension 1 integral subscheme.

> [!yellowbox]+ **Definition 1.2.3**: (Group of Weil Divisors $\Div X$)
> $\Div X$ is the free abelian group over the set of all prime divisors.
> Elements of $\Div X$ are called Weil Divisors on $X$

We want to think of Weil Divisors as carrying information about zeros and poles. So something like $2Z_1 - Z_2$ would mean having a zero of order $2$ along $Z_1$ and a pole of order $1$ along $Z_2$.

We make this idea more concrete after the following lemma.

> [!greenbox]+ **Lemma 1.2.4**: (Hartshorne II 6.1)
> Let $f$ be a non-zero rational function a $(*)$ scheme $X$, i.e., $f\in K(X)^\times$.
> Then for all but finitely many prime divisors, $v_Z(f) = 0$

^7a88ce

> [!proof]- _Proof_:
> Let $U$ be on open set on which $f$ is regular. Restricting to a smaller open set, we may assume $U$ is affine.
> \
> $X - U$ is a proper closed subset of $X$ and hence has only finitely many irreducible components. If $Z$ is a prime divisor that does not intersect $U$, then it would be one of these finitely many components, since by being codimension 1, it is already maximal irreducible.
> \
> If it intersects $U$, then its generic point $\eta_Z$ is in $U$. Further, $K(U) = K(X)$ and the valuations upon restricting to $U$ remain the same, i.e., $\nu_{U\cap Z} = \nu_Z$.
> So, to prove the lemma, it suffices to show that, $v_Z(f) \neq 0$ for only finitely many prime divisors that intersect $U$.
> \
> Since $f$ is regular on $U = \Spec A$, the valuation for $f$ will be non-zero at $Z\iff f\in m_Z \subseteq \SO_{X,Z} \iff f(\eta_Z) = 0 \iff Z\cap U \subseteq V(f)$
> \
> Again, $V(f)$ is a proper closed subset of $U$, hence has finitely many irreducible components, and any prime divisor that is complete contained in $V(f)$ will have to be one of them because it is codimension 1 and irreducible.

> [!yellowbox]+ **Definition 1.2.5**: (Principal Divisor)
> For a non-zero rational function $f \in K(X)^\times$ for a $(*)$ scheme $X$, define the principal divisor corresponding to $f$ as $(f) = \Sigma\nu_Z(f)Z$

This definition makes sense because of [[#^7a88ce|lemma]] 1.2.4

This construction gives a group homomorphism

$$
\begin{align}
K(X)^\times & \to  \Div  X \\
f &\mapsto (f) 
\end{align}
$$

This is a group homomorphism since $(fg) = \Sigma\nu_Z(fg) Z = \Sigma(\nu_Z(f) + \nu_Z(g))Z = (f) + (g)$
Thus the set of principal divisors form a subgroup of $\Div X$

> [!yellowbox]+ **Definition 1.2.6**: (Divisor Class Group $\Cl X$)
> $\Cl X := \Div X / \{\textnormal{Principal Divisors}\}$

Two divisor $D, D'$ are said to be linearly equivalent if they belong to the same class in $\Cl X$. This is denoted by $D \sim D'$ and is equivalent to saying $D- D'$ is a principal divisor.

> [!yellowbox]+ **Definition 1.2.7**: (Effective Divisor)
> A Weil Divisor $\Sigma a_Z Z$ is said to be effective is $a_Z \geq 0 \fall Z$

> [!greenbox]+ **Lemma 1.2.8**: (Effective Divisors on Normal spectrum)
> Let $A$ be a noetherian integrally closed domain and let $X = \Spec A$.
> Then for $f \in  K(X)^\times$, $(f)$ if effective $\iff$ $f\in A$

^d36b15

> [!proof]- _Proof_:
> Proof of backward implication:
> $f \in A \implies f\in A_p$ for all height one prime ideals $p$. So $\nu_p(f) \geq 0$ and hence $(f)$ is effective.
> \
> Proof of forward implication.
> For every height 1 prime ideal $p$, $\nu_p(f) \geq 0 \implies f \in A_p$
>
> $$
> f \in \bigcap_{\textnormal{ht 1 prime ideals}} A_p = A
> $$
>
> The last equality follows from [[Studies/Coursework/VSRP 2026/Final Writeup/Appendix/Commutative Algebraic Results#^6b98e8|Proposition]] CA.1

> [!greenbox]+ **Corollary 1.2.9**: (Kernel of $K(X)^\times \to \Div X$ for $X$ a Normal Spectrum)
> Let $A$ be a noetherian integrally closed domain and let $X = \Spec A$.
> $K(X) = K = \Frac A$. Then for $f \in K$, $(f) = 0 \iff f \in A^\times$

> [!proof]- _Proof_:
> Proof of backward implication:
> If $f \in A^\times$, then $f \in A_p^\times\ \forall$ primes ideals $p$
> Hence for height 1 prime ideals, $\nu_p(f) = 0$, hence $(f) = 0$
> \
> Proof of forward implication:
> $f\in A$ follows from the [[#^d36b15|lemma]] 1.2.8.
> Now since $f \in A$, if $f$ was not a unit, then the $f$ would be contained in some height 1 prime ideal, since $A$ is noetherian. So, $v_p(f) > 0$ for some height 1 $p$, which contradicts $(f) = 0$. Hence $f \in A^\times$
> \
> Another way to look at this is $v_p(f) = 0 \implies \frac 1 f \in A_p$ for all height 1 primes. Hence $1/f \in A$ and so $f$ is a unit.

> [!greenbox]+ **Proposition 1.2.10**: (Hartshorne II 6.2) ($\Cl \Spec A$ for a UFD)
> Let $A$ be a Noetherian Domain.
> $A$ is a UFD $\iff$ $A$ is integrally closed and $\Cl \Spec A = 0$

> [!proof]- _Proof_:
> From this [[Studies/Coursework/VSRP 2026/Final Writeup/Appendix/Commutative Algebraic Results#^2b1231|lemma]] CA.2, we use the ht one prime ideals being principal as the definition of UFDs to show the equivalence.
> \
> Proof of forward implication:
> UFDs are integrally closed. Hence $\Spec A$ is Noetherian integral normal scheme, hence satisfies $(*)$. So, we can talk about Weil Divisors and $\Cl \Spec A$.
> \
> Let $Z$ be a prime divisor. It corresponds to a height 1 prime ideal. $Z = V(p)$ and by hypothesis, $p = fA$ for some $f \in A$. We now show that $(f) = Z$
> \
> For any $W \neq Z$ a prime divisor, we have $W = V(q)$ for a height one prime ideal $q \neq p$. $f\notin q$ since $f\in q \implies p\subseteq q$ which contradicts $q$ being height 1. So $f$ is a unit in $A_q$ and hence $\nu_W(f) = 0$.
> \
> Further, $f$ generates $p$, so it also generates $pA_p$. So $\nu_Z(f) = 1$. This shows that $(f) = Z$. Since every prime divisor is principal, we get that every divisor in principal.
> \
> Proof of backward implication:
> $A$ is integrally closed, noetherian, domain, hence $\Spec A$ satisfies $(*)$. We need to show that every height one prime ideal is principal.
> \
> Let $p$ be a height one prime ideal and $Z = V(p)$ a prime divisor. Since $\Cl \Spec A = 0$, we have that $Z = (f)$ for some $f \in K(\Spec A)$. And since $(f)$ is effective, from [[#^d36b15|lemma]] we have $f \in A$. And $\nu_p(f) = 1 \implies f\in pA_p$.
> So, $f \in pA_p\cap A = p$.
> \
> Now let $g \in p$. Hence $g \in pAp$ and $(g)$ is effective with coefficient of $Z$ greater than 0. So $(g/f) = (g) - (f)$ is an effective divisor. Hence, again by [[#^d36b15|lemma]], $g/f \in A \implies g\in (f)$. Thus $p = fA$

#### Sheaf of Weil Divisors

First thing to observe is that the $(*)$ condition descends to open sets of a $(*)$. We implicitly used this in the proof of [[#^7a88ce|lemma]] 1.2.4. So, for every open set $U \subseteq X$, we have $\Div U$.

For $V \supseteq U$, we can define a map $\Div U \to \Div V$ on prime divisors of $U$ as $Z \mapsto Z\cap V$ if $Z\cap V \neq \emptyset$ and $0$ if $Z\cap V = \emptyset$. This makes sense since $Z$ and $Z\cap V$ have the same generic point and hence the local rings, whether calculated as a point of $U$ or $V$ are the same. Thus $Z\cap V$ is codimension 1 $\iff$ $Z$ is codimension 1.

> [!yellowbox]+ Notation: Restricting a Divisor to an open set
> For a divisor $D \in \Div X$, applying the map above to get a divisor in $U$ is the restriction of $D$ to $U$, denoted by $D_U$

> [!greenbox]+ **Lemma 1.2.11**:
> Suppose $V \supseteq U \supseteq X$ a $(*)$ scheme. And $Z'$ a prime divisor of $V$. Then there exists a unique prime divisor $Z$ of $U$ such that $Z_V = Z'$

^e9b702

> [!proof]- _Proof_:
> \
> Take $Z$ to be the closure of $Z'$ in $U$. Since $Z'$ is closed in $V$, we get that $Z\cap V = Z'$.
> Remains to show that $Z$ is a prime divisor. Let $\eta$ be the generic point of $Z'$. Let $\bar\eta$ be the closure of this point in $U$. Then $Z' \subseteq \bar\eta$. hence $Z\subseteq \bar\eta$. But $\eta \subseteq Z' \subseteq Z$. Thus $Z = \bar\eta$. Thus $Z$ is irreducible closed and its local ring is dimension one because it was dimension one with respect to $V$.
> \
> Now suppose there is $W$, a prime divisor of $U$ such that $W\cap V = Z'$.
> $\eta\in W\implies Z\subseteq W$ but we know both $Z$ and $W$ are codimension $1$. Hence $Z = W$

> [!greenbox]+ **Proposition 1.2.12**: (Sheaf of Weil Divisors)
> The assignment $U \mapsto \Div U$ and restriction maps as above define a sheaf on $X$

> [!proof]- _Proof_:
> That it forms a presheaf is easy to see, since if $V_1 \supseteq V_2 \supseteq V_3$ and $Z$ a prime divisor in $\Div V_3$  then $(Z \cap V_2)\cap V_3 = Z\cap V_3$
> \
> Now Suppose $\{U_\alpha\}$ cover $U$. $D \in \Div U$.
> Denote $D_{U_\alpha}$ by $D_\alpha$.
> \
> Now suppose $D_\alpha = 0$ for all $\alpha$. For every prime divisor $Z$ of $U$, there must be one of the $U_\alpha$ that intersects it. The only prime divisor of $U$ that maps to $Z\cap U_\alpha$ is $Z$. And its coefficient being zero tells us that coefficient of $Z$ in $D$ is $0$. Thus $D = 0$
> \
> Now suppose we have a collection $\{D_\alpha\}$ with $D_\alpha \in \Div U_\alpha$ such that $D_{\alpha\beta} = D_{\beta\alpha}$.
> Pick any one of the indices, say $\alpha_0$. Let $Z_1,\dots, Z_n$ be prime divisors of $U$ appearing in $U - U_{\alpha_0}$. Let $U_{\alpha_1},\dots,U_{\alpha_n}$ be open sets such that $U_{\alpha_i}\cap Z_i \neq \phi$.
> \
> Define a divisor $D \in \Div U$ as follows. For each $Z$ that intersects $U_{\alpha_0}$, set its coefficient to be same as the coefficient of $Z\cap U_{\alpha_0}$ in $D_{\alpha_0}$. Since $D_{\alpha_0}$ is a divisor, this will contribute only finitely many nonzero terms to $D$ since to every prime divisor of $U_{\alpha_0}$ there is a unique divisor over it by the previous lemma. And for each of the $Z_i$, take coefficient of $U_{\alpha_i}\cap Z_i$ in $D_{\alpha_i}$.
> \
> Now for any $\beta$ in the indexing set, we must show that $D|_{U_\beta} = D_\beta$. For every prime divisor $Z$ of $U_\beta$ fix an open set $U_Z$ by setting $U_Z = U_{\alpha_i}$ if $Z \subseteq Z_i$ and $U_Z= U_{\alpha_0}$ otherwise.
> \
> There is a prime divisor $W$ of $U$ over $Z$. The coefficient of $W$ in $D$ comes from $D_{U_Z}$. And coefficient of $Z$ in $D_\beta$ is same as coefficient of $Z\cap U_Z$ in $D_{U_\beta\cap U_Z}$. But by the hypothesis that it is a gluing family, this is same as coefficient of $W\cap U_Z \cap U_\beta$. Which is same as coefficient of $W$ by construction.

**A Remark:** This proof can be simplified a lot by thinking of prime divisors as the generic points of codimension one integral subschemes instead of the subschemes themselves. Then Divisors are functions from this set of dimension one generic points to $\mathbb Z$ with finite support and restriction becomes literally restricting this function to the smaller number of generic points inside the open set. Noetherian hypothesis is used to show that gluing maintains the finite support property of these functions.

###### Behavior of Stalks and restricting to $\Spec \OXx$

There is this notion of restricting a Weil Divisor to $\Spec \OXx$ for some point $x \in X$

$\Spec \OXx$ can be identified as subset of $X$. Then given any integral closed subscheme $Z$ with generic point $\eta$, $Z\cap\Spec \OXx \neq ,\emptyset \iff \eta \in \Spec \OXx$ because $\Spec \OXx$ is closed under generalization.

So, we can take a divisor $D$ on $X$, and get a divisor on $\Spec \OXx$ by sending a prime divisor $Z$ to $Z\cap \Spec \OXx$  or $0$ depending on whether the intersection is empty. And similar to [[#^e9b702|lemma]] 1.2.11 there is a unique prime divisor of $X$ over every prime divisor of $\Spec \OXx$.

> [!yellowbox]+ Notation: The above construction defines a divisor $D_x$ of $\Spec \OXx$

> [!greenbox]+ **Proposition 1.2.13**:
> The stalks of the sheaf of Weil Divisors, denoted by $\Divx$ is isomorphic to $\Div \Spec \OXx$ and the map $\Div X \to \Div \Spec \OXx$ defined above factors through this isomorphism.

> [!proof]- _Proof_:
> The map $\Divx \to \Div \Spec \OXx$ is defined by taking an element $<D, U>$ of the stalk, and sending it to $D_x$. The reverse map $\Div \Spec \OXx \to \Divx$ is defined by sending a prime divisor $W$ with generic point $\eta$ to $<\bar\eta, X>$, where $\bar\eta$ is the closure of $\eta$ is $X$.
> \
> Assuming well-definedness, it is easy to see that the two maps are inverses of each other. $\Divx$ is generated by $<Z,X>$ as $Z$ varies over prime divisors of $X$. If $Z\cap \Spec \OXx \neq \emptyset$ then $<Z,X> \mapsto Z\cap \Spec \OXx$ which then gets mapped back to $<Z,X>$. If $Z\cap \Spec \OXx = \emptyset$, then $<Z,X>\mapsto 0$. But now, $Z^C$ is an open neighborhood of $x$ and restricting $Z$ to this neighborhood gives $0$. Hence $<Z,X> = 0$
> \
> Given a prime divisor $W$ of $\Spec \OXx$ with generic point $\eta$, it gets mapped to $<\bar\eta, X>$ and $\bar\eta$ is a prime divisor of $X$, which gets mapped back to $\bar\eta\cap \Spec \OXx = W$
> \
> Further, the map $\Div X \to \Div \Spec \OXx$ factors through is easy to see since the definition of the isomorphism relies on the map.
> \
> Now the well-definedness. Suppose $<D,U> = <D', U'>$.
> So, there's a neighborhood $V$ of $X$ contained in $U, U'$ such that $D_V = D'_V$.
> But any open neighborhood of $x$ contains all generic points of prime divisors on $\Spec \OXx$. So, on the prime divisors that matter, $D$ and $D'$ agree, so $D_x = D'_x$

> [!greenbox]+ **Corollary 1.2.14**:
> For $X$ a $(*)$ scheme, for any open sets $U \subseteq X$ and $f \in K(U) = K(X)$, the principal divisor generated by $f$ in $X$ and $U$ be denoted by $(f)_X$ and $(f)_U$ respectively. Then under restriction map of the sheaf of Weil Divisors, $(f)_X \mapsto (f)_U$
> \
> Also, $((f)_X)_x$ is same as the principal divisor generated by $f$ in $\Spec \OXx$

> [!proof]- _Proof_:
> This follows since for each prime divisor $Z$ of $X$ such that $Z\cap U$ is a prime divisor on $U$, the valuations are the same, so coefficients would be same. And by [[#^e9b702|lemma]] 1.2.11 again, the contribution is completely from $Z$. Similarly, the valuations of $Z\cap\Spec \OXx$ and $Z$ are also the same, so the two divisors are the same.

> [!bluebox]+ **Proposition 1.2.15**: (Part of Hartshorne II 6.11, continued [[Studies/Coursework/VSRP 2026/Final Writeup/1) Setup/1.2) Cartier Divisors#^a2f5ac|Proposition]] 1.2.10)
> Let $X$ be a noetherian integral separated noetherian scheme which is locally factorial (all local rings are UFDs). The given any Weil divisor $D$, there exists an open cover $\{U_i\}_{i\in I}$ and a collection of rational functions $\{f_i\}_{i\in I}$ such that $D_{U_i} = (f_i)_{U_i}$, i.e. $D$ is locally principal.
