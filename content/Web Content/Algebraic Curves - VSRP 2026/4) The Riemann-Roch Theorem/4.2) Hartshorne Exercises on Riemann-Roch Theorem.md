### 4.2) Hartshorne Exercises on Riemann-Roch Theorem

<hr>

> [!yellowbox] **Problem 4.2.1**: (Hartshorne Exercise IV 1.1)
> Let $X$ be complete non-singular curve, $P\in X$. Then there exists a non-constant rational function $f\in K(X)$, which is regular everywhere except at $P$

^14206f

> [!proof]- _Solution:_
> Suppose $X$ is a curve of genus $g$. Consider the divisor $(g+1)P$. Applying [[4.1) The Riemann-Roch Theorem#^1476d4|Riemann-Roch Theorem]], $l((g+1)P) = l(K - (g+1)) + g+1 +1 - g \geq 2$. Thus $|(g+1)P|\geq 1$.
> \
> There is an effective divisor $D  \neq (g+1)P$ such that $(g+1)P \sim D$
> Hence there is an $f\in K(X)^\times$ non-constant such that $(g+1)P + (f) =D$
> But $(f)$ is a degree $0$ divisor from [[3) Divisors on Curves#^7453ae|Lemma]] 3.7.\
> So, $(f)$ must have a pole and the pole can only be at $P$ because $D$ is effective.
> This $f$ works.

>[!yellowbox] **Problem 4.2.2**: (Hartshorne Exercise IV 1.2)
>Let $X$ be a complete non-singular curve and let $P_1,\dots,P_r \in X$ be point. Then there is a rational function $f\in K(X)$ having poles at each of $P_i$, and regular everywhere else.

^86cb70

> [!proof]- _Solution:_
> From [[#^14206f|Problem]] 4.2.1, we can have rational functions $f_1,\dots,f_r$ such that $f_i$ has a pole only at $P_i$.
> \
> Pick a $c_i\in k$ such that $f_i(P_j) +c_i \neq 0 \fall j\neq i$. We can find such $c_i$ since the set $\set{f_i(P_j)}_{j\neq i}$ is finite, while our field being algebraically closed, is infinite.
> Let $g_i = f_i + c_i$
> \
> Note that $g_i$ does not a zero at $P_j, j\neq i$
> Let $f = \prod_{i =1}^rg_i$. So, $(f) = \sum (g_i)$.
> Now for each $i$, coefficient of $P_i$ is negative in $g_i$ and zero in $g_j, j\neq i$.
> So $f$ will have poles at each of $P_i$ and nowhere else.

>[!yellowbox] **Problem 4.2.3**: (Hartshorne Exercise IV 1.3)
>Let $X$ be a non-singular curve which is not complete. Then $X$ is affine.

> [!proof]- _Solution:_
> Every non-singular curve is an open subset of a complete non-singular curve. Let that curve be $\tilde X$
> \
> $\tilde X - X$ will be finite set of points $P_1,\dots,P_r$. Get a rational function $f$ which has poles only at these points using [[#^86cb70|Problem]] 4.2.2.
> \
> Consider the map $\tilde f: \tilde X \to \PP^1$ given by $f$ using [[3) Divisors on Curves#^2f3d23|Lemma]] 3.6. This is finite by [[3) Divisors on Curves#^be3f40|Proposition]] 3.3 and $X = \tilde f \inv (\PP^1 - (1:0))$. $\PP^1 - (1:0) \simeq \AA^1$. Thus $X$ is affine.

>[!yellowbox] **Problem 4.2.4**: (Hartshorne Exercise IV 1.5)
>For an effective divisor $D$ on a curve $X$ of genus $g$, show that $\Dim |D| \leq \deg D$. Furthermore, equality holds if and only if $D = 0$ or $g =0$

> [!proof] _Solution:_

By [[4.1) The Riemann-Roch Theorem#^1476d4|Riemann-Roch Theorem]] $\Dim |D| = l(D) - 1 = l(K-D) + \deg D- g$

> [!greenbox]+ **Lemma 4.2.4.1**:
> Let $X$ be a complete non-singular curve. $D, D'$ divisors with $D$ an effective divisor. Then $l(D'- D)\leq l(D')$

> [!proof]- _Proof_:
> Since $D$ is effective, $\SL(-D)$ is an ideal sheaf. i.e., we have
>
> $$
> 0 \to \SL(-D) \to \OX
> $$
>
> Tensoring by $\SL(D')$, which is flat, because it is locally free, we get
>
> $$
> 0 \to \SL(D'-D)\to \SL(D')
> $$
>
> Which tells use $\Gamma(X,\SL(D'-D)) \subseteq \Gamma(X, \SL(D))$ and thus $l(D'-D)\leq l(D)$

From the lemma above, we get $l(K-D) \leq l(K) = g$. So the inequality follows.

For $D = 0$, $l(K-D) = l(K) = g$, so equality follows. And for $g = 0$, $K-D$ has negative degree, so $l(K-D) = g = 0$. Thus again, equality follows.

If neither of these holds, then the following lemma does the job

> [!greenbox]+ **Lemma 4.2.4.2**:
> Let $X$ be a complete non-singular curve of genus $g>0$ and $P\in X$ a point. Then $l(K-P) < l(K)$. Consequently, for any effective divisor $D$, $l(K-D) < l(K)$

> [!proof]- _Proof_:
> Note that $\Dim |P| = 0$ since if we have any other effective divisor of degree $1$, it would correspond to a point $Q\neq P$ and $P\sim Q$. But this would mean $X\sim \PP^1$ by [[3) Divisors on Curves#^71aa25|Lemma]] 3.8 and $g = 0$, which is a contradiction.
> \
> So, by [[4.1) The Riemann-Roch Theorem#^1476d4|Riemann-Roch Theorem]],
> $1 - l(K-P) = 1 + 1 - g\implies l(K-P)  = g - 1 < g = l(K)$

Since $g > 0$, the lemma above applies and we get $\dim |D| < \deg D$

>[!yellowbox] **Problem 4.2.5**: (Hartshorne Exercise IV 1.6)
>Let $X$ be a curve of genus $g$.  Show that there is a finite morphism $f: X\to \PP^1$ of degree $\leq g+1$

> [!proof]- _Solution:_
> Pick any point $P\in X$. As in [[#^14206f]], there is a non-constant rational function such that $(g+1)P + (f)$ is an effective divisor. $f$ can have a pole only at $P$ and of order at most $g+1$. Say the order of pole is $n \leq g+1$
> \
> Considering the map $\tilde f: X\to\PP^1$ by $f$, $\tilde f ^*((1:0)) = nP$. Thus $f$ is a finite morphism of degree $n \leq g+1$

>[!yellowbox] **Problem 4.2.6**: (Hartshorne Exercise IV 1.7 (a))
>A complete non-singular curve $X$ is called hyperelliptic if $g \geq 2$ and there existsa finite morphism $f: X\to \PP^1$ of degree $2$
>\
>If $g = 2$, then show that the canonical divisor defines a complete linear system $|K|$ of degree $2$ and dimension $1$, without base points. Conclude that every curve of genus $2$ is hyperelliptic.

> [!proof]- _Solution:_
> Firstly, $\deg K = 2g - 2 = 2$
> And $l(K) = g = 2$. Thus $\Dim |K| = 1$
> \
> Now, for any point $P\in X$, by lemma in 4.2.4, $l(K- P) < l(K)$
> Thus $\Dim |K-P|$ is $0$ or $-1$
> But $|K-P|$ is exactly the divisors $D - P$ which are effective and $D\in |K|$
> $|K-P|$ being smaller that $|K|$ forces that there must be divisors $D\in |K|$ such that $D- P$ is not effective, i.e., $P\notin \Supp D$. Thus the complete linear system $|K|$ is without a base point.
> \
> Picking any basis $s_0, s_1$ of $|K|$, the map defined by linear system in the one induced by $\frac {x_0} {x_1} \mapsto$ "$\frac {s_0} {s_1}$" which corresponds to the divisor $(s_0)_0 - (s_1)_0$. $(s_0)_0$ will be a divisor in $|K|$, thus it will be of degree $2$ and it will correspond to the pre-image of $(0:1)$. Thus the map will be of degree $2$.

> [!yellowbox] **Problem 4.2.7.1**: (Hartshorne Exercise IV 1.8 (a))
> Show that $p_a(X) = p_a(\tilde X) + \Sigma_{P\in X}\ \delta_P$

> [!proof]- _Solution_
> Using [[2) Sheaf Cohomological Results#^def235|Proposition]] 2.9, we can conclude that $\chi(f_*\SO{\tilde X}) = \chi(\SO_{\tilde X})$.
> \
> Applying [[2) Sheaf Cohomological Results#^970f48|Lemma]] 2.5 to the exact sequence above, we get
> $\chi(\SO_{\tilde X}) = \chi(\OX) + \sum_{P\in X}\chi(\tilde \SO_{X,P}/\SO_{X,P})$. The latter are skyscraper sheaves at closed points, so by [[2) Sheaf Cohomological Results#^a50a53|Corollary]] 2.7, this becomes
> $\chi(\SO_{\tilde X}) = \chi(\OX) + \sum_{P\in X}\delta_P$
> \
> Since these are curves, $p_a(X) = 1 - \chi(\OX)$ and similar of  $\tilde X$
> So, $p_a(X) = 1 - \chi(\SO_{\tilde X}) + \sum_{P\in X}\delta_P = p_a(\tilde X) + \sum_{P\in X}\delta_P$ >

> [!yellowbox] **Problem 4.2.7.2**: (Hartshorne Exercise IV 1.8 (b))
> If $p_a(X) = 0$, show that $X$ is already nonsingular and is in fact isomorphic to $\PP^1$

> [!proof]- _Solution:_
> Let $\tilde X$ be normalization of $X$, then by the above problem, we must have $\delta_P= 0$ for all $P$. Thus $X  = \tilde X$ and $p_a = p_g = g$. From [[4.1) The Riemann-Roch Theorem#^b88ce6|Proposition]] 4.1.9,  $X\sim \PP^1$

>[!yellowbox] **Problem 4.2.8**: (Hartshorne Exercise IV 1.9(a)) Riemann-Roch for Singular Curves
>Let $X$ be a projective curve and let $\Xreg$ be the set of regular points of $X$.
>Let $D = \Sigma \ n_iP_i$ be a divisor with support in $\Xreg$. Then define $\deg D = \Sigma \ n_i$. 
>Let $\SL(D)$ be the associated invertible sheaf on $X$, and show that $\chi(\SL(D)) = \deg D + 1 - p_a$

^f77bab

> [!proof]- _Solution:_
> Let $\pi: \tilde X \to X$ be a normalization of $X$. Then $\pi\inv(\Xreg) \to \Xreg$ is an isomorphism.
> \
> Let $\varphi: K(X) \to K(\tilde X)$ denote the isomorphism between the fields induced by $\pi$.
> \
> $\Xreg$ is a Noetherian normal scheme. Hence by [[1.2) Cartier Divisors#^a2f5ac|Proposition]] 1.2.10, $D|_{\Xreg} = \set{(f_i, U_i)}$.
> Let $S$ denote the points of $\Xreg$ which have a non-zero coefficient in $D$.
> Extend the Cartier divisor above to a Cartier divisor $C$ on $X$ by describing it using the collection: $\set{(f_i,U_i), (1, X-S)}$. $\SL(D) = \SL(C)$ by definition.
> \
> Now, $\pi$ is a finite dominant map. So we pullback $C$ to a divisor $\pi^*C$. $\pi^*C$ is described as $\set{(\varphi(f_i),\pi\inv(U_i)), (1,\tilde X - S)}$.
> $\SL(\pi^*C)$ is a line bundle on $\tilde X$. Now push it forward to $X$ again to get $\pi_*\SL(\pi^*C) = \SF$.
> \
> We now give an injective map from $\SL(C) \to \SF$
> For each open set $U$, $\Gamma(U, \SL(C)) \subseteq K(X)$. Also, $\SF(U) = \Gamma(\pi\inv(U), \SL(\pi^*C)) \subseteq K(\tilde X)$.
> Send $g \in \Gamma(U, \SL(C))$ to $\varphi(g) \in K(\tilde X)$.
> \
> On the open sets $U_i$, this map sends $g = \frac {g_i} {f_i} \in \frac 1 {f_i} \SO_{U_i}$ to $\frac {\varphi(g_i)}{\varphi(f_i)} \in \frac 1 {\varphi (f_i)} \SO_{\pi\inv(U_i)}$. Similar holds for $X-S$
> Since upon restricting to these open sets, the map lands inside $\SF$, the full map also lands inside $\SF$.
> \
> We will now see what the cokernel $\SF/\SL$ of this inclusion is.
> Upon restricting to $U = U_i$ or $X-S$, the sequence becomes
>
> $$
> 0 \to \SO_U\to \pi_*\SO_{\pi\inv(U)}\to (\SF/\SL)|_{U} \to 0
> $$
>
> Where the map $\SO_U \to \SO_{\pi\inv(U)}$ is $\varphi|_{\SO_U}$ since the original maps came from $\varphi$ and to get isomorphism to the structure sheaves, we multiply by $f_i$ and $\varphi(f_i)$ respectively.
> The cokernel is then known to be $\sum_{P\in U}\tilde\SO_{X,P}/\SO_{X,P}$
> From this, we get that $\SF/\SL$ is supported only at the points of singularity and by [[Sheaf Theoretic Results#^e7b717|Lemma]] SH.2, we have $\SF/\SL \simeq \sum_{P\in X}\tilde\SO_{X,P}/\SO_{X,P}$
> \
> Finally, we get the following exact sequence:
>
> $$
> 0 \to \SL(C)\to \SF \to \sum_{P\in X}\tilde\SO_{X,P}/\SO_{X,P} \to 0
> $$
>
> So, $\chi(\SL(C)) = \chi(\SF) - \sum_{P\in X}\delta_P$
> But $\SF = \pi_*(\SL(\pi^*C))$. Thus $\chi(\SF) = \chi(\SL(\pi^*C))$ by [[2) Sheaf Cohomological Results#^def235|Proposition]] 2.9.
> Further, the Weil divisor corresponding to $\pi^*C$, denoted by $\pi^*D$ will have the same degree as $D$ since it is only supported on $\Xreg$ and $\pi\inv(\Xreg)\to \Xreg$ is an isomorphism.
> \
> Using [[4.1) The Riemann-Roch Theorem#^1476d4|Riemann-Roch Theorem]] on $\tilde X$, we get $\chi(\pi^*(C)) = \deg D + 1 - p_a(\tilde X)$
> Substituting this in the relation above and using [[#^1bd440|Problem]] 4.2.7, we finally get
> $\chi(\SL(C)) = \deg D +1 - p_a(\tilde X) - \sum_{P\in X}\delta_P = \deg D +1 - p_a(X)$

>[!yellowbox] **Problem 4.2.10**: (Hartshorne Exercise IV 1.10)
>Let $X$ be a projective curve which is locally a complete intersection and has $p_a = 1$.
>Fix a point $P_0\in \Xreg$. Show that the map $P\to \SL(P-P_0)$ gives a one-to-one correspondence between the points of $\Xreg$ and the elements of the group $\PicO X$

> [!proof]- _Solution:_
> _**What is $\PicO X$**_?
> \
> Since $X$ is an integral scheme, we have an isomorphism $\Pic X \simeq \CaCl X$. Every Cartier divisor on $X$ need not correspond to a Weil Divisor, because at the singular points the local ring is not a DVR. But we can still define a degree function on $\CaCl X$ by pulling back the divisor to $\CaCl \tilde X$, where $\pi: \tilde X \to X$ is the normalization of $X$.
> \
> From this, we get a degree map $\Pic X \to \CaCl X \to \CaCl \tilde X \to \ZZ$.
> Let $\PicO X$ be the kernel of this map. That this definition is consistent with the definition in [[#^f77bab|Problem]] 4.2.8 and the Riemann-Roch for singular curves can be applied to all divisor classes in $\Pic X$ for this particular problem is shown [[#^9c9f8f|here]].
> \
> **Claim:** For the curve as above, $K \sim 0$
> Applying the formula from [[#^9bab81|Problem]] 4.2.9, for $D = 0$ we get $\deg K =0$ and for $D = K$ we get $l(K) = 1$. If $D$ is a non-zero Cartier divisor in $\Gamma(X, \SL(D))$, then $D = \set{(f_i, U_i)}$ is effective and degree zero. So $\pi^*D$ is effective and degree zero and so $\pi^*D = \set{(f_i, \pi\inv (U_i))}$. Whenever $\tilde x \mapsto x\in U_i$ under $\pi$, the $f_i \in \OXx$ (it is in $\OXx$ because we started with an effective divisor) maps to a unit in $\SO_{\tilde X, \tilde x}$. Hence $f_i$ must be unit for all $i$. And so we get $D$ is trivial and so $K \sim D \sim 0$
> \
> **Claim:** For $D \in \Pic X$ of negative degree, $l(D) =0$
> Degree of $D$ being negative means degree of $\pi^*D$ is negative. If $D'$ is an effective divisor equivalent to $D$, then $\pi^*D'$ is an effective divisor equivalent to $\pi^*D$. But since $\deg \pi^*D < 0$, there cannot be such. Hence $l(D) = 0$
> \
> Now, let $D$ be divisor of degree $0$. Then $D+P_0$ is a divisor degree $1$.
> We get $l(D+P_0) - l(-D-P_0) = 1+1-1 \implies l(D+P_0) = 1$ because $l(-D-P_0) = 0$ from the previous claim.
> \
> Let this divisor be $C = \set{(f_i, U_i)}$. Then $\pi^*C$ is an effective degree 1 divisor. So, $\pi^*C = Q$ for some $Q \in \tilde X$.
> \
> Suppose for now, that $\pi(Q) = P$ is in $\Xreg$. Then $C|_{\Xreg} = P$. And since on the singular point, $\pi^*f_i$ is a unit in its preimage, otherwise that would appear in the expression of $\pi^*C$. And so $f_i$ is also also a unit in local ring of the singular point, because it also known to be regular. So we can replace it with $1$ in the Cartier divisor and conclude that $C = P$ as defined in [[#^f77bab|Problem]] 4.2.8.
> \
> Further, if $C = P'$ for some $P' \in \Xreg$, then $\pi^*C = \pi\inv(P')$ because at all points other than the ones above $P'$, the representative for $C$ is a unit. So, it remains a unit after pullback. So we get $\pi\inv(P') = Q$ and $P = P'$.
> \
> So, there is a unique $P \in \Xreg$ such that $D \sim P - P_0$ and so $P \mapsto \SL(P-P_0)$ establishes a bijection $\Xreg$ to $\PicO X$.
> \
> So, if we show that $\pi^*C = Q$ for $Q\in \pi\inv(\Xreg)$ we would be done.
> \
> Since $X$ has arithmetic genus $1$, there is exactly one point of singularity $S$. If there are more than one points lying over it, then if $C$ is zero at $S$, $\pi^*C$ will be supported at all the preimages of $S$ and we would get a contradiction to it being degree $1$. If there is exactly one point over $S$, then the next [[#^52aa9c|lemma]] tells us the map must be ramified. So $\pi^*C$ will have degree at least $2$, giving us a contradiction.  Thus, $C$ is not zero at $P$.

> [!greenbox]+ **Lemma**: (Hartshorne II 7.4)
> Let $f: A\to B$ be a local homomorphism of Noetherian local rings, such that
> \
> (1) $A/\fm_A \to B/\fm_B$ is an isomorphism
> (2) $\fm_A \to \fm_B/\fm_B^2$ is surjective
> (3) $B$ is finitely generated $A$ module
> \
> Then $f$ is a surjective.

^658a75

> [!proof]- _Proof_:
> Let $I = \fm_AB$. We'll first show $I = \fm_B$.
> A basis for $\fm_B/\fm_B^2$ gives a generating set for $\fm_B$ as an ideal of $B$ since $B$ is local Noetherian ring. But since the map $\fm_A\to \fm_B/\fm_B^2$ is surjective, image of $\fm_A$ in $B$ contains a generating set of $\fm_B$, and so $I = \fm_B$.
> \
> Now looking at $B$ as an $A$ module, $B/\fm_AB = B/\fm_B \simeq A/\fm_A$. So, $\bar 1$ generates $B/\fm_AB$ as an $A$-module. Since $B$ is finitely generated, by Nakayama, $1$ generates it as an $A$ module. Thus $f(A) = B$ and we are done.

> [!greenbox]+ **Lemma**:
> Let $X$ be a curve and $\pi: \tilde X \to X$ be its normalization. If $P \in X$  is a singular point such that $\pi\inv(P)$ is a singleton, then for $Q = \pi\inv P$, the map of local rings $\pi^*:\OXP \to \SO_{\tilde X, Q}$ is ramified, i.e., $\pi^*(\fm_P) \subseteq \fm_Q^2$

^52aa9c

> [!proof]- _Proof_:
> Since $Q$ is the only point over $P$, $\SO_{\tilde X, Q}$ is the integral closure of $\OXP$. And since normalizations of varieties are finite, we get that $\SO_{\tilde X, Q}$ is a finite $\OXP$-module.
> \
> Since these are closed points of varieties over algebraically closed fields, the residue fields at these are isomorphic. If the map is unramified, then $\fm_P \to \fm_Q/\fm_Q^2$ is surjective.
> \
> Hence by [[#^658a75|lemma]] above, we have $\OXP \to \SO_{\tilde X, Q}$ is a surjection, and hence an isomorphism. This is a contradiction to $P$ being a singular point.

> That the degree expression is same in both the cases is easy to see.

> We get the formula:
